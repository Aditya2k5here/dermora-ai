How it works

Four stages, every one of them visible to you

Nothing in the pipeline is hidden behind a single number. Each stage reports its own result, and the interface tells you when they disagree.

01

Quality gate

Focus, exposure, contrast, glare and skin-tone coverage are measured before anything else. An unusable image is flagged rather than quietly scored.

02

Classification

A YOLOv8 network scores all seven classes across every anchor. Scores are pooled and converted into a true probability distribution, averaged over four mirrored views of the image.

03

Explanation

Eigen-CAM projects the network's internal features into a heat map, so you can confirm it looked at the lesion and not at a hair, a ruler or the background.

04

Second opinion

Classical image analysis measures the lesion's asymmetry, border, colour and structure and scores the ABCD rule of dermoscopy — completely independently of the network.

Self-examination

The ABCDE rule, in plain language

What dermatologists teach patients to look for between appointments. Dermora scores the first four automatically; only you can assess the fifth.

A

Asymmetry

Fold the mole in half in your mind. If the two halves do not match, note it.

B

Border

Edges that are ragged, notched, blurred or scalloped rather than smooth and clean.

C

Colour

More than one shade — browns, black, red, white or blue-grey in the same lesion.

D

Diameter

Larger than 6 mm, about the width of a pencil eraser. Melanomas can be smaller, so size alone never rules it out.

E

Evolving

Any change over weeks or months in size, shape, colour, or new itching, bleeding or crusting. This is the single most important sign — and no single photograph can assess it.

The basics

Skin cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer group in the world. It is usually divided into two families. Keratinocyte cancers — basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma — are far more common and rarely fatal, but can destroy local tissue if neglected. Melanoma is much rarer, accounting for a small minority of skin cancers, yet it causes the majority of skin-cancer deaths because it can spread early.

The single most important variable in melanoma outcome is thickness at diagnosis. A thin, early melanoma is usually cured by a straightforward excision. The same tumour caught later has a substantially worse prognosis. That gap is precisely why screening tools exist, and why the cost of a false alarm is small compared with the cost of a missed lesion.

What this means for a screening tool. The thresholds in Dermora are deliberately tuned towards sensitivity. It would rather send you to a dermatologist unnecessarily than reassure you incorrectly.

The seven classes

These are the diagnostic categories in the ISIC 2018 Task 3 dataset, which is what this model was trained on. Expand any class for its dermoscopic pattern and management.

What dermoscopy adds

A dermatoscope is a magnifier with a polarised light source held against the skin. Polarisation cancels the surface glare that normally hides everything below the stratum corneum, so structures inside the lesion — pigment networks, globules, vessels, blue-white veils — become visible.

In trained hands dermoscopy substantially improves diagnostic accuracy for melanoma over naked-eye examination. In untrained hands it can be worse than nothing, which is the gap that automated analysis is trying to close.

Ordinary phone photographs are out of distribution. Every training image was captured through a dermatoscope. A normal photo of a mole will still produce a result, but that result is much less trustworthy, and the quality gate will usually say so.

Risk factors

  • Fair skin that burns rather than tans, light eyes, red or blond hair
  • A history of blistering sunburn, particularly in childhood
  • High cumulative ultraviolet exposure, occupational or recreational
  • Any use of sunbeds or tanning booths
  • A large number of moles, or several atypical or dysplastic ones
  • A personal or first-degree family history of melanoma
  • Immunosuppression, including after organ transplantation
  • Increasing age, though melanoma also affects young adults

Darker skin is not immune. Melanoma in richly pigmented skin more often appears on the palms, soles and nail beds, and is frequently diagnosed later — which is exactly the population this model's training data covers least well.

Prevention

  • Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ daily on exposed skin, reapplied every two hours outdoors
  • Shade between late morning and mid-afternoon, when UV peaks
  • Hats, long sleeves and UV-blocking sunglasses beat sunscreen alone
  • No sunbeds, at any age, for any reason
  • A monthly self-check in good light, including scalp, soles and between toes
  • Photograph anything you are unsure about, so change is measurable later

When to see a doctor

Book an appointment — do not wait for a screening tool to agree with you — if any of the following apply:

  • A mole that has changed in size, shape or colour over weeks or months
  • A new pigmented lesion appearing after roughly age 30
  • A lesion that itches, bleeds, crusts or fails to heal within four weeks
  • The "ugly duckling" — one lesion that looks unlike all your others
  • A dark streak appearing under a fingernail or toenail without injury
  • Any lesion that simply worries you, regardless of what any tool says
Dermora cannot rule anything out. A benign, low-concern result is not clearance. Only a clinician who has examined the lesion in person — and, where needed, histopathology — can do that.
